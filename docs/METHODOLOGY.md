# 1. Research design

## 1.1 What kind of research is this?

This study is **quantitative, computational, simulation-based comparative experimental
research**. Each of those four words is doing work and none of them is decorative:

**Quantitative.** Every claim is a number with units, produced by a stated procedure and stored
in a CSV file under `results/`. There is no qualitative or interpretive component. Where the
report says "the model fails", it means a specific error metric crossed a specific threshold.

**Computational.** The objects of study are algorithms and their outputs, not physical
apparatus. No measurements were taken from the world. This is the same category of work as a
numerical study in computational physics, and it inherits the same standard of evidence:
convergence must be demonstrated, not assumed.

**Simulation-based.** The data are generated by simulation rather than observed. This is
a deliberate methodological choice with a specific justification, set out in §5.3, and a
specific cost, set out in §10.2.

**Comparative experimental.** The core structure is a controlled experiment in the classical
sense: one factor is deliberately varied, everything else is held constant, and the response is
measured. The study is comparative along two axes at once — between **models** (five families)
and between **physical systems** (projectile and planetary).

## 1.2 Why a simulation study is a legitimate research design here

The obvious objection is that synthetic data is "not real", and it deserves a direct answer.

The research question is not *"how does a projectile move?"* — that was settled in the
seventeenth century. The question is *"how does a class of learning algorithm behave when
applied to a dynamical system, and how does that behaviour depend on the character of the
system?"*. The object of study is the **algorithm**, not the projectile.

For studying an algorithm, having exactly-known ground truth is not a weakness but the
essential enabling condition. It is what makes it possible to say that a rollout error of
0.01 AU is entirely the model's fault, with no measurement noise, no unmodelled forces and no
integrator error to argue about. An experimental physics dataset would introduce all three and
would make the central measurements of Stages 8, 11 and 12 impossible to attribute.

The analogy is to a benchmark in numerical analysis: one tests an integrator on a problem whose
answer is known, precisely so that the discrepancy can be attributed to the integrator.

## 1.3 The controlled-comparison principle

Every experiment in this study follows one rule, stated here once:

> **Change exactly one thing at a time, and report what was held fixed.**

Examples of how this rule shaped the design, each of which was a real decision rather than an
afterthought:

- Stage 7 varies training-set size. The test set is split off **once**, before the loop, and is
  identical for every model at every size. Otherwise a change in RMSE could not be attributed
  to the size.
- Stage 8 varies the region of parameter space the **test** data comes from. The training set is
  a single fixed set of 8,000 rows used by all five models in all seven regimes.
- Stage 12 compares two physical systems. Because Part I used a closed-form map and Part II used
  a stepped flow map, a direct comparison would confound the system with the formulation. So
  projectile motion was **re-expressed as a one-step flow map** (§5.4) purely so that the
  formulation could be held fixed and the physics varied alone.
- Stage 13 compares a PINN against a plain network. The plain network is the *same class* with
  `physics_weight = 0` — same architecture, same initialisation, same optimiser, same epochs.
  The only difference is the loss.

# 2. Research question and sub-questions

## 2.1 Main research question

> **How effectively can machine learning approximate and predict physical motion, and how does
> its predictive performance differ between projectile motion and planetary dynamics?**

## 2.2 Sub-questions actually investigated

The project proposal listed seven candidate sub-questions. Five were retained, two were
reformulated, and the reasoning is recorded here because deciding what *not* to study is part of
the methodology.

| # | Sub-question | Status | Where answered |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Can an ML model accurately predict projectile trajectories? | **Retained** | Stages 5–6 |
| 2 | How does training-data size affect accuracy? | **Retained** | Stages 7, 11.5, 12.2 |
| 3 | How far into the future can the model accurately predict? | **Retained, and made central** | Stages 8.3, 11.2 |
| 4 | Can the same approach be applied to planetary motion? | **Retained** | Stage 11 |
| 5 | Does complexity lead to greater error? | **Reformulated** — see below | Stage 12 |
| 6 | How does ML compare with classical computation? | **Retained** | Stages 6, 10, 12.4 |
| 7 | How does error accumulate over time? | **Retained, and made central** | Stages 11.2, 12.1 |

**Why sub-question 5 was reformulated.** "Does complexity lead to greater error?" is not
answerable as stated, because "complexity" is undefined and because the two systems have
different units, so their errors cannot be compared at all without normalisation. It was
replaced with a question that can be answered by measurement:

> With the problem formulation, model family, training-set size and error normalisation all held
> fixed, how does the **relative** prediction error of a learned flow map differ between a system
> whose exact flow map is affine and one whose exact flow map is not?

This version names the structural property (affine vs not) that the study can actually test,
and it is what Stage 12 measures.

**Why sub-questions 3 and 7 were promoted.** During Stage 11 it became clear that error
accumulation in autoregressive rollout is not one finding among several — it is the dominant
phenomenon separating the two systems, and the only one that produces a difference of many
orders of magnitude. The report is organised around it accordingly.

## 2.3 Objectives

1. Implement a validated classical simulator for each system (Stages 1, 10).
2. Generate reproducible datasets from those simulators with documented sampling design (§5).
3. Train and evaluate a common set of regression models on both systems (§6).
4. Quantify accuracy, generalisation, error accumulation and physical consistency (§7).
5. Determine where and why the learned approach fails, rather than reporting only where it works.
6. Test whether embedding physical law into the model changes those failure modes (Stage 13).

## 3.3 Controlled variables (held fixed)

- **g = 9.81 m/s²** and **GM = 4π² AU³/yr²** throughout. Never fitted, never varied.
- **Random seeds** are fixed and stated for every generator, split and model.
- **The test set is reserved before any model is trained**, in every experiment.
- **Model hyperparameters** live in `src/models.py` and `src/planetary_ml.py` and are identical
  across every arm of every comparison. They are module-level constants precisely so that this
  is checkable.
- **Hardware**: all timings on one machine (Apple Silicon, macOS, Python 3.12.8, single process).
  Timings are comparative, not absolute benchmarks.
- **Library versions**: NumPy 2.2.1, pandas 2.2.3, scikit-learn 1.6.1, matplotlib 3.10.0.

## 3.4 Confounds identified and the design change made for each

Listing these is more useful than claiming there were none.

| Potential confound | Why it would matter | What was done |
|---|---|---|
| Different test sets across training sizes | RMSE change could be the test set, not the size | Single fixed test set, split once (Stage 7) |
| Formulation differing between the two systems | System-vs-system difference would be confounded with map-vs-rollout | Projectile re-expressed as a flow map (Stage 12) |
| Incomparable units (m vs AU) | No meaningful comparison possible at all | All errors normalised by system scale, all times by natural period |
| Ground truth itself carrying error | ML error could be reference error in disguise | Reference validated against the analytic Kepler solution to ~10⁻¹³ AU (§4) |
| One-step pairs crossing trajectory boundaries | A few physically impossible transitions get learned as if real | Shifts performed strictly within each `traj_id` |
| PINN being a bigger/longer-trained model | Improvement would be capacity, not physics | Plain network is the same class with `physics_weight = 0` |
| A single lucky random draw | Result would not be reproducible | 5 repeats (Stage 7), 3 seeds (Stage 13), 20 test trajectories (Stages 11–12) |
| Cherry-picked illustrative trajectory | Figure would not represent the typical case | The Stage 7 trajectory figure uses the test row nearest the **median** launch speed, and pairs the single-trajectory panel with an average over 40 conditions |

# 4. The ground truth, and how it was validated

A study that measures error must first establish that its reference is right. The two systems
required different arguments.

## 4.1 Projectile motion — exact by construction

x(t) and y(t) are closed-form algebraic expressions. The reference is exact to floating-point
precision; there is nothing to validate beyond the algebra, which is derived from Newton's
second law in the report's theory section.

## 4.2 Planetary motion — validated against the analytic Kepler solution

The generic eccentric Kepler orbit requires a numerical root solve rather than an elementary
explicit r(t). This project also integrates the equations in time, so the reference trajectories
have numerical error. That error was checked rather than assumed, in four independent ways:

1. **Against the analytic Kepler solution.** Position as a function of time *is* obtainable for a
   Kepler orbit by solving E − e sin E = M numerically for the eccentric anomaly (Newton–Raphson,
   tolerance 10⁻¹⁴) and mapping to Cartesian coordinates. This is implemented in
   `kepler_solution()` and is an *independent* route to the answer — no time stepping involved.
   RK4 agrees with it to **4.6 × 10⁻¹⁴ AU (e = 0), 1.3 × 10⁻¹³ AU (e = 0.3), 8.1 × 10⁻¹³ AU
   (e = 0.6)** over two full orbits.

2. **Convergence order.** Measured slopes of log(error) vs log(Δt), sampled a quarter of the way
   round an orbit: **0.974, 0.994, 1.994, 3.917** for Euler, Euler–Cromer, Verlet and RK4,
   against theoretical 1, 1, 2, 4. A wrong slope is the standard signature of an implementation
   error; these are right.

3. **Conserved quantities.** Over 200 orbits, Velocity Verlet holds ǀΔE/E₀ǀ ≤ 1.5 × 10⁻⁸ with no
   secular trend, and angular momentum to ~10⁻¹⁴. Explicit Euler, by contrast, gains 80% of its
   energy — the failure is real and is reported, not hidden.

4. **Kepler's third law.** Orbital periods measured from simulations across a = 0.4–5.0 AU give a
   fitted log–log slope of **1.500000** against Kepler's 3/2. The simulator was only ever told
   Newton's inverse-square law; the third law emerged from the dynamics, in the historically
   correct direction of inference.

**Resulting error budget.** The reference used for all Stage 11 experiments (RK4, Δt_store =
0.004 yr with 10 internal substeps) differs from the analytic solution by **≈ 3 × 10⁻¹¹ AU** over
the whole 5-year horizon. The smallest ML error measured anywhere in Stage 11 is 6.4 × 10⁻⁵ AU —
**six orders of magnitude larger**. The reference is therefore exact for present purposes, and
that statement is quantitative rather than rhetorical.

## 4.3 Validating the hand-written neural network

The PINN in `src/pinn.py` implements forward propagation, backpropagation and Adam by hand, so
its gradients were verified against central finite differences of the loss:

| Loss term | Max gradient error | Relative |
|---|---|---|
| Data loss | 6.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ | — |
| Physics loss | 6.2 × 10⁻⁶ | 8.6 × 10⁻⁷ of the largest gradient |
| Initial-condition loss | 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ | — |

The physics-loss figure is larger in absolute terms because that loss contains a factor 1/h² =
10⁴, which amplifies both the true gradient and the finite-difference noise; the *relative* error
of 10⁻⁶ is the correct floor for this kind of check.

# 5. Data generation

## 5.1 Projectile dataset

Each row is an **independent** sample, not a point along a shared trajectory:

| Variable | Distribution | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| v₀ | Uniform(5, 50) m/s | Spans a thrown ball to a fast sports projectile |
| θ | Uniform(5°, 85°) | Avoids the degenerate horizontal and vertical limits |
| y₀ | Uniform(0, 20) m | Ground level to a rooftop launch |
| t | Uniform(0, t_flight) **per sample** | See below |

**The key sampling decision** is that t is drawn within *each sample's own* flight time rather
than from a fixed global range. If t were drawn from, say, Uniform(0, 6 s), then a slow, low
launch would be sampled mostly after it had already landed — generating rows with y < 0 that
describe a projectile continuing below the ground. Sampling as a fraction of each flight keeps
every row physical and samples every trajectory evenly across its own duration.

**Features** [v₀, θ, y₀, t] — these are the quantities an observer could know before the flight,
plus the time asked about. **Targets** [x, y] — the quantities to be predicted.

Sizes: 2,000 rows (Stages 3–6), 20,000 rows (Stage 7), 8,000 + 7 × 2,000 (Stage 8).

## 5.2 Planetary dataset

Orbits are generated in astronomical units (AU, year, solar mass), in which GM = 4π² exactly.
Working in SI would mean squaring numbers of order 10¹¹ inside every energy calculation and
discarding precision for no benefit.

| Variable | Distribution | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| a | Uniform(0.8, 1.2) AU | A narrow band, so the model is not asked to extrapolate in a |
| e | Uniform(0.0, 0.4) | Circular through moderately elliptical; e > 0.4 has a periapsis passage too fast to resolve at this Δt |
| Initial phase | Always apoapsis on the +x axis | The slowest, most gently curving point — a fixed-step integrator's easiest starting regime, so any later breakdown is attributable to periapsis |

60 training and 20 test trajectories, 3 orbits each, sampled every Δt = 0.004 yr (250 points per
1-year orbit), giving 44,285 one-step training pairs.

**Δt_store vs Δt_integrate.** These are separated deliberately. Δt_store = 0.004 yr is the
interval the *ML model* learns to step over; it must be large or a five-orbit rollout would need
tens of thousands of model calls. Δt_integrate = Δt_store/10 is the interval the *integrator*
uses; it must be small or the ground truth would carry visible error. Using one value for both
would force a bad compromise.

## 5.3 Why synthetic data is valid here — and what it costs

**Valid because:** the object of study is the algorithm; exactly-known truth is what makes error
attributable; and the alternative (real trajectory measurements) would add noise, air resistance
and calibration error, each of which would have to be disentangled before any of the questions in
§2.2 could be asked.

**The cost, stated plainly:** a model trained on data from an equation inherits every assumption
of that equation. Specifically, this study's projectile data assumes constant g, no air
resistance, flat ground and a point mass; its orbital data assumes a fixed central body, Newtonian
gravity, no perturbations and exactly two bodies. A model that performs well here has learned to
reproduce **a simulation**, not nature. Nothing in this report claims otherwise. Real projectiles
with air resistance follow a noticeably non-parabolic path, and Mercury's orbit precesses by
43″/century in a way Newtonian gravity cannot account for.

## 5.4 Projectile motion re-expressed as a dynamical system (for Stage 12)

To compare the two systems fairly, projectile motion is also generated in state-sequence form —
s = [x, y, vₓ, v_y] sampled every Δt — so that the identical one-step flow-map formulation can be
applied to both. The exact projectile flow map is

```
x'  = x + vₓ·Δt
y'  = y + v_y·Δt − ½g·Δt²
vₓ' = vₓ
v_y' = v_y − g·Δt
```

Every line is **affine** in the state. This is the structural fact that drives the whole of
Stage 12, and it is verifiable: fitting ordinary linear regression to these pairs at Δt = 0.05 s
recovers the coefficient matrix to machine precision, with intercepts −0.0122625 = −½g·Δt² and
−0.4905 = −g·Δt. The orbital flow map contains 1/r³ and lies inside no model's hypothesis space.

# 6. Models

## 6.1 The families, and why each is present

| Model | Role in the study | Key property |
|---|---|---|
| **Linear Regression** | Baseline | Cannot represent products of inputs; its failure quantifies how much of the problem is non-linear |
| **Polynomial Ridge (deg 2)** | Intermediate baseline | Can form pairwise products (v₀t, t²) but not cos θ |
| **Random Forest** | Flexible, assumption-free | Piecewise constant → excellent interpolation, structurally incapable of extrapolation |
| **MLP (64,64 tanh)** | Flexible, smooth | Included so neural networks are tested rather than dismissed |
| **Physics features + linear** | Hard-constraint reference | Exact by construction; the ceiling everything else is measured against |
| **PINN (32,32 tanh, NumPy)** | Soft-constraint reference | Needs only the ODE, not its solution |

## 6.2 Why not "just use a neural network"

The project proposal explicitly warned against reaching for deep learning because the project is
about AI. The design honours that by starting with the simplest model that could work and adding
complexity only where a measurement justifies it. Two findings vindicate the ordering:

- Linear regression's *failure* in Part I is informative, not merely a poor score: it fails
  because the true mapping contains v₀cos(θ)t, and knowing that is what motivates everything
  after it.
- In Stage 12, linear regression is not just adequate for the projectile flow map — it is
  **exact**, at ~10⁻¹⁴ m. Any more complex model there would be strictly worse and much slower.
  A study that had opened with a neural network would have missed this entirely.

## 6.3 Absolute vs delta targets

For flow-map models the target may be the next state s' or the change Δs = s' − s. Delta form is
used throughout, for a reason that can be checked: over one step the planet moves ~0.025 AU while
sitting at ǀrǀ ≈ 1 AU, so in absolute form the model must produce ~1.0 to a precision of 10⁻⁴ and
spends nearly all its capacity reproducing the identity map. In delta form the identity is free.

Measured effect on one-step position error: Random Forest 2.5 × 10⁻² → 8.7 × 10⁻⁴ AU (29×);
MLP 1.8 × 10⁻³ → 6.4 × 10⁻⁵ AU (28×); linear and polynomial **unchanged to 10 significant
figures** — as they must be, since a linear model can already represent the identity and the
reparametrisation is one it absorbs exactly.

# 7. Evaluation metrics

## 7.1 Definitions

For n test samples with true values yᵢ and predictions ŷᵢ:

| Metric | Formula | Units | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| MAE | (1/n)Σǀyᵢ − ŷᵢǀ | same as y | Typical error |
| MSE | (1/n)Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² | y² | Penalises large errors quadratically |
| RMSE | √MSE | same as y | Back in physical units, still worst-case dominated |
| R² | 1 − SS_res/SS_tot | — | 1 = perfect, 0 = no better than the mean, < 0 = worse than the mean |
| Radial error | ‖(x,y) − (x̂,ŷ)‖₂ | same as y | Coordinate-free distance |

**RMSE ≥ MAE always**, and a large gap indicates a heavy-tailed error distribution. Both are
reported for this reason.

**R² is reported but never alone.** In Stage 8 several models attain negative R² — which is the
metric working correctly, telling us the model is worse than a constant predictor, but which is
easy to mistake for a bug if seen without context.

## 7.2 Metrics specific to dynamical prediction

**Usable horizon.** The first time the rollout error exceeds a stated tolerance, by linear
interpolation between steps. Tolerances: 0.001, 0.01 and 0.1 AU (0.1%, 1% and 10% of 1 AU). This
is the operational form of "how long is this forecast good for?".

**Relative energy drift ǀΔE/E₀ǀ.** Computed from the model's *own* predicted trajectory. Its
value is that it requires no ground truth at all — the true system conserves E exactly, so any
drift is error, measurable even where no reference exists.

**Physics residual.** ǀd²x/dt²ǀ and ǀd²y/dt² + gǀ of the model's own output, by central finite
difference with h = 0.01 s, applied identically to every model. This asks a question RMSE cannot:
*is the predicted motion physically possible at all?* The two questions come apart sharply — the
Random Forest achieves 2.65 m position error with a residual of ~2,000 m/s², because its output
is piecewise constant in t and so has wild numerical curvature.

## 7.3 Normalisation for cross-system comparison

Metres and AU cannot be compared. Stage 12 divides every error by the system's own characteristic
length (horizontal range of the flight; semi-major axis of the orbit) and every time by its own
natural period (flight time; orbital period). The resulting quantities are dimensionless and one
unit on either axis means the same physical thing in both systems.

# 8. Experimental protocols, stage by stage

Each protocol is stated as: manipulation → measurement → control.

### Stage 5–6 — Baseline evaluation
80/20 split of 2,000 rows, `random_state=42`. Train linear and forest; report MAE/RMSE/R² per
coordinate; plot actual-vs-predicted, residuals, and a swept trajectory. *Control:* identical
split for both models.

### Stage 7 — Effect of training-set size
Sizes {100, 500, 1000, 5000, 10000} × 5 random draws each × 2 models, on a 20,000-row dataset.
*Control:* the 4,000-row test set is split off once, before the loop, and never trained on. Report
mean ± SD over draws; also measure whole-trajectory reconstruction error averaged over 40 unseen
launch conditions, because a single trajectory is too noisy to judge convergence by eye.

### Stage 8 — Interpolation vs extrapolation
One fixed 8,000-row training set from v₀ ∈ [15,40] m/s, θ ∈ [20°,70°], y₀ ∈ [0,15] m. Seven test
regimes × 2,000 rows: one drawn from the same box (the control) and six pushing one or all
variables outside it. Plus: a continuous sweep of test v₀ from inside to far outside; a
forward-in-time test (train on the first 60% of each flight, test across all ten deciles); and a
1-D response slice along which the true answer is exactly linear, so each model's deviation *is*
its inductive bias made visible. *Control:* one training set, five models, all seven regimes.

### Stage 10 — Integrator validation
Four integrators × nine step sizes × two sampling times, against the analytic Kepler solution.
Plus 200-orbit conservation runs and a Kepler's-third-law check across seven semi-major axes.
*Control:* identical initial conditions and identical orbit count for every method.

### Stage 11 — ML for orbital motion
Three formulations (A/B/C) × two target parametrisations × four model families, rolled out 1,250
steps over 20 unseen orbits. Diverged rollouts (ǀrǀ > 100 AU) are recorded as escaped rather than
dropped, so an unstable model is not silently flattered. *Control:* one fixed set of training
trajectories and one fixed set of test initial conditions for every arm.

### Stage 12 — System vs system
The same formulation and model families, with 20,000 training pairs in each system. The fixed step sizes are 0.02 s and 0.004 yr; the number of
steps per natural timescale is not matched. One-step and learning-curve scores use the training
pool (with overlap); rollout tests use separate trajectories. See report Section 12 for these
qualifications. *Control:* the shared flow-map formulation.

### Stage 13 — Physics-informed learning
Label counts {0, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 2000} × {PINN, plain} × 3 seeds. *Control:* the plain
network is the same class with `physics_weight = 0` — same architecture, initialisation,
optimiser, learning rate, epochs and collocation points.

# 9. Reproducibility

## 9.1 Determinism

Every stochastic step is seeded: dataset generation (`np.random.default_rng(seed)`), train/test
splits (`random_state`), model initialisation (`random_state` / `seed`), and subset draws. Scientific metrics should reproduce within numerical tolerance in the recorded environment.
Timing columns vary with machine load, so CSVs containing timings are not byte-identical.

## 9.2 Full reproduction

```bash
python run_all.py          # regenerates every dataset, result and figure
```

Individual stages can be run alone; see `README.md` for the ordering and approximate runtimes.

## 9.3 What is stored

- `data/` — every dataset as CSV
- `results/*.csv` — every number that appears in the report
- `results/*.png` — every figure, regenerated from those numbers
- `src/`, `plots/`, `experiments/` — the code, with the derivations in the docstrings

No figure in this report was produced by hand or adjusted after generation.

# 10. Threats to validity

Stated as threats with mitigations, including the ones that could not be fully mitigated.

## 10.1 Internal validity

| Threat | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Test-set leakage | Test rows split off before training in every experiment; one-step pairs never cross a trajectory boundary |
| Hyperparameters tuned on the test set | No tuning was performed at all. Hyperparameters are fixed module constants, chosen once from standard defaults, never selected against test performance. This costs some absolute accuracy and buys an uncompromised test set |
| Single random draw | 5 repeats (Stage 7), 3 seeds (Stage 13), 20 test trajectories (Stages 11–12), with spread reported |
| Reference error masquerading as model error | Reference validated to 10⁻¹¹ AU vs ML errors ≥ 10⁻⁵ AU (§4.2) |
| Hand-written gradients being wrong | All three loss gradients checked against finite differences (§4.3) |

## 10.2 External validity — the honest limits

1. **Synthetic data.** Conclusions apply to learning the *simulated* systems. Real projectiles
   experience drag; real planets experience perturbations and relativistic precession.
2. **Two systems only.** Both are integrable and neither is chaotic. The single most important
   untested case is a genuinely chaotic system (three-body, double pendulum), where error growth
   would be exponential rather than polynomial and the conclusions might differ in character.
3. **Two dimensions, one central body.** No N-body interactions, no orbital resonances.
4. **No noise.** Every dataset is noise-free. A noisy-data replication is the most valuable single
   extension and is named as such in the conclusions.
5. **Modest model scale.** The largest network is ~35k parameters trained on ~44k samples. The
   published results this study reviews use models orders of magnitude larger. Where a conclusion
   might be scale-dependent, the report says so.
6. **Known improvements deliberately omitted.** Noise injection during training
   (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al. 2020), multi-step rollout losses (Lam et al. 2023), Hamiltonian
   architectures (Greydanus et al. 2019) and Neural ODEs (Chen et al. 2018) are all known to
   improve exactly the failure mode measured in Stage 11. They were left out so that the baseline
   phenomenon could be measured cleanly. **The Stage 11 rollout numbers are therefore a baseline,
   not a state-of-the-art result**, and must not be quoted as evidence of what learned simulators
   can achieve.

## 10.3 Construct validity

Does "prediction error" capture "has the model learned the physics"? **No, and that gap is itself
a finding.** It is why Stage 13c measures the physics residual separately, and why Stage 11
measures energy drift. A model can score well on position error while describing impossible
motion — the Random Forest does exactly that.

## 10.4 Statistical conclusion validity

The comparisons here are of means over small numbers of repeats, reported with standard
deviations or interquartile ranges. **No formal hypothesis tests are performed**, because the
effects that matter are separated by several orders of magnitude, where a significance test adds
nothing. Where two results are close (e.g. PINN vs plain network at 2,000 labels: 0.43 ± 0.05 m
vs 0.36 ± 0.06 m), the report says they are **equivalent within the spread of the seeds** rather
than declaring a winner.

# 11. Ethics, integrity and scope of claims

## 11.1 Claims this study makes

1. On these simulated systems, standard regression models interpolate accurately and extrapolate
   poorly, with the failure mode determined by the model's inductive bias.
2. Autoregressive rollout of a learned flow map accumulates error rapidly, and the accumulation —
   not per-step accuracy — determines the usable horizon.
3. The difference between the two systems is structural: the projectile flow map is affine and
   lies inside linear regression's hypothesis space; the orbital flow map does not lie inside any
   tested model's.
4. Embedding physical law reduces the data requirement substantially and improves physical
   consistency, with the benefit largest where data is scarce or absent.

## 11.2 Claims this study explicitly does **not** make

1. **That ML outperforms classical physics on these systems.** It does not — by roughly ten
   orders of magnitude. RK4 holds ~10⁻¹¹ AU over five years; the best learned rollout exceeds
   0.01 AU within about a third of one orbit.
2. **That any model discovered a physical law.** Every model was trained on data generated *from*
   the law or constrained by it. Physics features recover an exact form here; the PINN is
   approximate. Neither is evidence of discovering the equation.
3. **That these results transfer to real experimental data**, to chaotic systems, or to
   model scales far larger than those tested.
4. **That the Stage 11 rollout numbers represent what learned simulators can do.** See §10.2.6.

## 11.3 Data and code integrity

No result was discarded for being inconvenient. Two in particular were investigated rather than
smoothed over, and both became findings:

- **Euler–Cromer measured as second order** when its formal order is one. Rather than adjusting
  the fit, the error was measured at a quarter period as well as a full period; the discrepancy
  turned out to be the known conjugacy of symplectic Euler to Störmer–Verlet, and it is now
  reported as a result with its explanation.
- **The polynomial model's rollout diverged to infinity**, crashing the pipeline. Rather than
  excluding the model, divergence was made an explicitly recorded outcome (NaN beyond 100 AU),
  because an unstable model must not be flattered by having its worst runs dropped.

All literature citations were checked against publisher records before inclusion. No paper,
author, DOI or result in `docs/LITERATURE_REVIEW.md` is invented.
